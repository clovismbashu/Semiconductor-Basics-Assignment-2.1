Question 1

 Difference between n-type and p-type semiconductor materials

An n-type semiconductor is formed by doping a pure semiconductor (such as silicon or germanium) with pentavalent impurity atoms (e.g., phosphorus or arsenic). These impurities contribute extra electrons, making electrons the majority charge carriers and holes the minority carriers.

A p-type semiconductor is formed by doping the semiconductor with trivalent impurity atoms (e.g., boron or gallium). These impurities create a deficiency of electrons, resulting in holes as the majority charge carriers and electrons as the minority carriers.

Question 2

Forward-bias and reverse-bias conditions of a p–n junction diode and their effect on current

In forward bias, the p-type region of the diode is connected to the positive terminal of the supply and the n-type region to the negative terminal. This reduces the width of the depletion region and lowers the potential barrier, allowing charge carriers to cross the junction easily. As a result, a significant current flows through the diode.

In reverse bias, the p-type region is connected to the negative terminal and the n-type region to the positive terminal. This increases the width of the depletion region and raises the potential barrier, preventing majority carriers from crossing the junction. Only a very small leakage current (due to minority carriers) flows.

Question 3

 Meaning of the word “ideal” as applied to a device or system

When the term ideal is applied to a device or system, it refers to a theoretical model that operates perfectly according to assumptions, without losses, imperfections, or limitations. An ideal device simplifies analysis by ignoring real-world effects such as resistance, leakage current, power loss, or temperature dependence.

For example, an ideal diode conducts current with zero voltage drop in forward bias and blocks all current in reverse bias.
